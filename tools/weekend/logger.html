How to use it
Not connected
Browser storage is unavailable or full. Keep this page open until it says saved online: data on this page will not survive a reload.

Connect
WiFi, from a phone
  1. Turn on your phone's hotspot. On iPhone, switch on Maximize Compatibility: the board only sees 2.4 GHz.
  2. In the flasher, flash the gateway with your hotspot's name and password, then scan its QR code with this phone. That sets Gateway ID here and connects. On a Heltec, use the Batear flasher with MQTT URL mqtt://broker.hivemq.com:1883.
  3. Tap Connect over WiFi.
USB cable, from a laptop
  1. Chrome or Edge on a laptop. Phones can't read a USB board from a web page yet.
  2. Plug the gateway in, tap Connect USB, pick its port. Tap again to add the detector, which adds its confidence score.

WiFi mode sends readings through a free public broker, so anyone who guessed the gateway ID could read them. Fine for a backyard test, not for anything sensitive. Keep this page open with the screen on: readings that arrive while the phone is locked are lost.

Speaker test
  1. Pair a Bluetooth speaker with this phone. Silent mode off, Do Not Disturb on, Auto-Lock off. Put the speaker on a chair 16 ft from the mic, facing it, with the gateway beside it. Keep this phone beside the mic, with its mic on (Sound at the mic).
  2. Pick a sound, tap Play calibration, and use the volume buttons until Sound at the mic reads 60–65 dB(A). Then don't touch the volume again.
  3. Tap Measure at the mic. The phone reads 10 s with the speaker off, then 10 s of calibration, and fills both readings in. Or type them from any sound meter.
  4. Tap Start sequence and walk 65 ft behind the speaker. You have 60 s before the first play. The phone then starts recording, plays each distance once (each is logged as a Speaker run), records the background as a No drone run and stops the recording, about 10 minutes in all.

What the numbers mean

  
Sound

  

    
dB, decibels

    
The scale for how loud something is. It counts in steps, not amounts: 10 dB more sounds about twice as loud, and 6 dB less is what you get each time you double your distance from the sound.

    
dB(A)

    
Loudness the way a sound meter reads it, weighted like your ears, which hear deep rumble and very high hiss less. This is the loudness at mic number. For a feel of it:
      

        
30a quiet bedroom at night

        
40a quiet room, a library

        
50a quiet office, light rain

        
60talking, 3 ft away

        
70a vacuum cleaner

        
80a busy street, at the curb

      

    

    
dBFS, the raw level

    
How loud the sound is inside a microphone's own recording, where 0 is the loudest it can record. So it is always negative: about −90 is near silence and −20 is loud. Two devices give different dBFS for the same sound, so it only tells you if a sound got louder or quieter for that one device. The detector reports dBFS: detector hears.

    
Setting the meter

    
Turns the phone's raw level into dB(A). Phone mics differ by several dB, so once per phone you read a steady sound, such as a fan, with a sound meter app on the same phone, then type that reading in here while the same sound plays. Until then the logger shows the raw level in orange.

    
Background

    
The level with nothing playing: wind, birds, traffic. A drone has to rise above it to be heard, so a quiet day makes for a longer range.

  

  
The speaker test

  

    
Drone loudness

    
The total sound a drone gives off, from its official noise rating. The logger uses it to work out how loud that drone would be at the mic from each distance.

    
Emulated distance

    
Instead of flying a drone 650 ft away, the speaker plays its recording quietly enough to sound like it at the mic. Each doubling of distance is 6 dB quieter.

    
Calibration

    
A steady sound at the speaker's full test volume. Its reading at the mic is the starting point the logger turns down from for each distance.

    
Aimed, measured, detector

    
In the tables, Aimed is the level the logger tried to play at the mic, Measured is what the phone at the mic heard, and Detector is what the detector heard, in its own raw level. If aimed and measured are far apart, that play didn't stand in for the distance it claims.

  

  
Radio and detection

  

    
Alarm, Batear

    
The detector's software, Batear, decides "drone" and raises an alarm, which the listener sends by radio.

    
Detector sure, 0–1

    
How sure the detector is that the sound is a drone. Only shown when the detector is plugged in by USB.

    
Gateway

    
The board that receives the listeners' radio messages and passes them to this page.

    
Packet

    
One radio message from a listener: an alarm, or a check-in once a minute that says it's alive and how loud it is there.

    
Radio signal, dBm

    
How strong the listener's radio message was when it arrived (RSSI). Closer to zero is stronger: −40 is very strong, −100 is fine, and about −120 is the limit.

    
Radio clarity, dB

    
How far the message stood above radio noise (SNR). LoRa radio still works below zero, down to about −15.
