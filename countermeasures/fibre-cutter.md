# The fibre cutter
{: .no_toc }

<p class="vz-lede">A fence wire about 3 ft off the ground, with a small trolley on each span. A fibre FPV's line falls across whatever lies under the drone's path, so where the drone crosses, its line lands <strong>on our wire</strong>. When the tripwire hears it, the trolley runs along the wire and cuts the line in about 4 seconds, <strong>with nobody exposed</strong>.</p>

<details class="layer" id="short-answer" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Short answer</span><span class="layer-sub">The whole page in eight bullets</span></summary>

- **The idea is sound, and others have done half of it.** A fence that hears a drone and breaks its line already exists twice in Ukraine: a turning barbed wire, and Logika's Chaharnyk ([Countermeasures]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link countermeasures/index.md %}#whats-already-out-there)). Ours is a trolley on a farm-fence wire, set off by the tripwire's alerts.
- **The wire makes sure the line is on it.** A drone pays out its line with no tension from 65–165 ft up, so where it crosses the wire, the line lands across it. No dart has to work out where the fibre lies.
- **One trolley per 165 ft span.** On longer spans the wire sags too close to the grass. Each trolley parks at its span's post on a solar charger.
- **A cut takes about 4 s on average, under 7 s at worst** (1 s to start, then 20 mph along the span). Chaharnyk claims 1 s, because its whole fence moves at once. Our trolley is cheaper and slower.
- **Best on a road.** A drone parked to wait gives the cutter all the time it needs. Against a drone flying in, the wire has to be at least 500 ft in front of what it protects.
- **Parts cost about $3.6k a mile in a small batch, sold at about $9.7–12.7k.** Upkeep is about $600–1,100 a mile a year, plus trimming the grass under the wire (est.).
- **Set up by hand, like a farm fence:** about a mile a night for a crew of two (est.). A drone can't lay a tight wire, so the forward line a mile out stays listen-only.
- **Two rules:** never cut our own fibre (ours crosses the wire only at a post, where no trolley goes), and hold the trolleys while our own fibre drones are out.

<details class="layer" id="why-it-lands-on-the-wire" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Why the line lands on the wire</span><span class="layer-sub">The fibre's path, and where it doesn't hold</span></summary>

The fibre runs without a break from the pilot to the drone, and it falls from 65–165 ft up. So wherever the drone crosses our wire, the line must pass either over the wire or under it. Falling from above, **it ends up lying across the top of the wire.** That's why this design strings a wire rather than hunting for a line lying somewhere on the ground: the wire decides where the line will be.

**Where that fails:**
- **In tree belts.** A line that crosses in the canopy hangs in the branches, well above a 3 ft wire. Put the cutter across open fields and leave gaps at windbreaks. A drone that flies down a tree belt slips through.
- **Wind** can carry a slack line sideways as it falls, but not over the whole length of the wire. It still has to cross the wire somewhere, so it lands on another span.
- **Old line already on the ground** from earlier drones lies under the wire, not over it, and does no harm.
- **Not yet tested:** whether a line laid from a real drone, over real grass, in wind, ends up lying on a 3 ft wire. This is test C1 ([below](#what-has-to-be-tested)).

</details>

<details class="layer" id="the-design" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">The design</span><span class="layer-sub">Wire, spans, posts, the trolley and its blade</span></summary>

**The wire is 12.5 ga high-tensile smooth fence wire,** the farm kind. A 4,000 ft roll costs about $140–165 and weighs 99 lb, and the wire breaks at about 1,500 lb ([Tractor Supply](https://tractorsupply.com/tsc/product/high-tensile-smooth-wire-200-000-psi-4-000-ft), [Do it Best](https://6370-cdn.doitbest.com/shop/farm-and-ranch/fencing-and-gates/fence-wire-rope-and-tape/high-tensile-wire/oklahoma-steel-and-wire-4000-ft-125-ga-high-tensile-steel-high-tensile-wire?SKU=738660)). It is set to 250 lb with a ratchet strainer, and a tension spring keeps it tight through a 100°F swing between summer and winter. (That swing changes the tension by about ±70 lb, our arithmetic.) A 1/16 in aircraft cable is lighter, but it breaks at 480 lb and can only carry about 96 lb, so it sags more ([Tractor Supply](https://www.tractorsupply.com/tsc/product/tie-down-1-16-in-7x7-galvanized-steel-aircraft-cable-1-000-ft-spool)).

**Spans are 165 ft (50 m).** A trolley can't pass a post, so each span gets its own trolley. Span length is a trade between sag and trolleys (model):

| Span | Sag: wire + trolley | Lowest point, from 3 ft at the posts | Trolleys a mile | Cut, mean / worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **165 ft** | 0.34 + 0.33 ft | **2.3 ft** | **32** | **3.8 / 6.6 s** |
| 330 ft | 1.35 + 0.66 ft | 1.0 ft, in the grass | 16 | 6.6 / 12.2 s |

**Posts** are 6 ft steel T-posts, about $10 each ([Traffic Safety Store](https://www.trafficsafetystore.com/shop/6-ft-steel-fence-posts-no-spade-lite-weight-0-95-lbs-linear-foot/TPOST-6-LW-G-NS.md)): one at each span end, and three-post braces at the ends of the line and at any bend, about 4 a mile. In-line posts carry the wire's weight only, because the tension is balanced along a straight run.

**The trolley** is a cable cam without a camera. Sports filmers already run these on a line: a Wiral LITE runs at about 21 mph on 164 ft of line, weighs 1.3 lb and runs for 3 hours ([Wiral](https://eu.wiralcam.com/products/wiral-lite), [B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1452771-REG/wiral_lite_cable_camera_for_any.html)). Ours hangs from the wire on three V-groove wheels. It has:
- a drone motor and ESC driving a 2 in grooved wheel through a belt,
- a dart's board (ESP32-S3 and LoRa) that hears the net's alerts,
- a 3S pack of 18650 cells,
- a magnet and Hall sensor at each end stop,
- **a ceramic blade deep in a V-notch at each end.** A thin line is guided down the V into the blade. A finger or a branch is too wide to reach it, the way a seatbelt cutter works.

A cut takes far less force than the trolley has: the fibre breaks at about 2–13 lbf. Glass is hard enough to dull steel quickly, so the blades are ceramic and are changed twice a year (est.).

**It reports each cut.** A jolt on the blade, seen by the board, sends "cut on span 14, 120 ft from the post". That confirms the cut, and the line points back toward its pilot.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="how-fast" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">How fast it cuts, and where to put it</span><span class="layer-sub">Seconds to the cut, the distance a drone covers meanwhile, and what that means for placement</span></summary>

The model drops a crossing at a random point on a span. The trolley starts 1 s after the drone passes, which is Chaharnyk's claimed response, and runs at 20 mph, a little under a Wiral's 21 mph.

| 165 ft span | Mean | 9 in 10 | Worst | Drone at 43 mph travels meanwhile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trolley parked at the post (the design)** | 3.8 s | 6.1 s | 6.6 s | 240 / 380 / 420 ft |
| Trolley parked mid-span | 2.4 s | 3.5 s | 3.8 s | 150 / 220 / 240 ft |

Parking mid-span is faster, but it needs a solar panel on the trolley and a charging stop in the middle of the wire. That's left as an option.

**What that means for placement:**
- **Drones waiting by a road: no limit.** The road guard's posts hear every landing within 300 ft of the road ([Guarding a road]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/route-guard.md %})). A wire about 330 ft out on the enemy side lies between every such drone and its pilot. The "drone waiting" alert sets off its span, and nothing is in a hurry.
- **Drones flying in: at least 500 ft in front of what it protects.** By the time the line is cut, the drone has come 240–420 ft past the wire. A drone that goes dark should come down short of its target, but it falls with its warhead, so the wire also has to sit far enough from people. A drone with a backup radio keeps coming, onto the radio and the jammers.
- **Wait, then run.** The tripwire hears a drone about a mile out and names the stretch, so the trolleys there are ready a minute early. They should wait at their posts and run when the posts or darts hear the drone pass overhead. Sweeping back and forth instead passes any point only every 11 s or so, which is slower. Sweeping is for drones that crossed unheard.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="laying-it" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Laying it</span><span class="layer-sub">Who sets it up, how long it takes, and why a drone can't</span></summary>

**It goes up like a farm fence, by hand, from a vehicle, at night,** the same way the road guard's posts do. Per mile, for a crew of two (est.):
1. **Mark the line** on open ground about 330 ft out from the road, with a gap at each tree belt. About 30 min with a map and a range finder.
2. **Drive the posts:** about 41 T-posts with a hand driver, about 3 min each, about 2 h.
3. **Brace the ends** and any bends, about 4 a mile: about 1 h.
4. **Run and tension the wire** off a payoff spinner, one ratchet and spring per run: about 2 h.
5. **Hang the trolleys,** clip the solar panel and charger to each parking post, and pair each trolley to the nearest road post: about 32 × 5 min, about 2.5 h.

**That's about 8 h, about a mile a night for two people (est.).** A wire across the threat side of a 4 mi road is about four crew-nights. Farm fencers are faster.

**Why not lay it from a drone, a mile out?** A trolley needs a tight wire, and a tight wire needs posts, braces and a person to tension it. A drone can trail a slack cord between two dropped anchors, but a slack cord lying in grass can't carry a trolley. Untested idea: two winch darts saw the slack cord back and forth across a few yards, so anything lying across it is worn through. That runs into drag and snags over hundreds of feet of grass and is not part of this design. The forward line a mile out stays listen-only.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="power" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Power</span><span class="layer-sub">What a trolley uses, and the solar at its post</span></summary>

- **A sweep, out and back along 165 ft, uses about 0.1 Wh** (model: speeding up and braking each way, rolling resistance, 50% drive efficiency).
- **A day of hourly sweeps plus the board listening is about 3 Wh.** The 3S 18650 pack holds about 27 Wh, about 9 days with no sun.
- **A 10 W panel on the parking post** charges the trolley through two contacts when it parks. In midwinter, about 0.5–1 hours of full sun a day still gives 5–10 Wh (est.; T12's winter test covers the darts' panels, not this one).
- Hourly sweeps are a choice. A weekly sweep and alert-only runs would need almost no solar.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="keeping-it-up" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Keeping it up</span><span class="layer-sub">Blades, cells, grass, ice, shelling, and the old line it collects</span></summary>

A year, a mile, at small-batch prices (model, est.):

| Item | A year | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Blades | ~$260 | Glass dulls an edge. Both blades swapped spring and autumn |
| Packs | ~$150 | 18650s replaced every 3 years |
| Trolleys lost | ~$150–590 | 5–20% a year to shelling, vehicles and theft; a pure guess |
| Wire splices, re-tensioning | ~$25–100 | A wire cut by shrapnel is spliced and re-tensioned in minutes |
| **Total** | **~$600–1,100** | plus labour |

**Not in the money:**
- **Grass and weeds.** Anything growing up to the wire snags the trolley. A 3 ft strip under the wire needs trimming about twice a summer, or a weedkiller.
- **Ice.** Freezing rain glazes the wire, and grooved wheels can slip on it. A sweep every hour in icing weather should knock thin ice off. Thick glaze stops the trolley until it thaws. Not yet tested (C3).
- **Old line.** Each sweep cuts any line lying across the wire, including the dead ones from earlier drones. Bits of fibre left on the wire are harmless, but should be cleared at each blade change so the wheels don't wind them up.
- **A daily roll call.** Each trolley runs once a day and reports, the same way the darts check in, so a jammed or missing trolley shows up the same day.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="our-own-fibre" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">Our own fibre, our own drones, and people</span><span class="layer-sub">What the cutter must never cut, and keeping people and animals safe</span></summary>

- **Our tip darts' fibre.** On a road, every 2 mi a tip dart's fibre runs from the fence to a post ([Guarding a road]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/route-guard.md %})). It must cross the cutter wire at a post, never mid-span, because no trolley passes a post. The crew lays the cutter after the fibre and puts a post where the fibre is.
- **The unit's own fibre FPVs.** Any of our fibre drones flying out past the wire leaves its line across it. **The cutter can't tell their line from the enemy's.** Two rules: the trolleys only run on alerts for drones heard *inbound* (the tripwire's [inbound-only rule]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link models/tripwire.md %}#solving-the-field-limits-at-no-extra-cost), proposed, T15), and a **hold** set from the checkpoint stops all sweeps on a stretch while our own sorties are out. Troops already cut every line they find, friend or foe. A cutter that holds while our own sorties fly does better than that.
- **People and animals.** The blade sits deep in a narrow V, so nothing wider than a cord reaches it. But a 2 lb trolley at 20 mph is still a hazard at shin height. Mark the wire, keep it off paths and gates, and keep it out of livestock fields. A cow will push it down.
- **Our own movement.** A wire across a field blocks our own vehicles and people, which is one critique of Chaharnyk too. On a road it runs beside the road, not across it, with a gate wherever a track crosses. The gate is a span with no trolley, and drones can slip through it.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="what-it-misses" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">What it misses</span><span class="layer-sub">Drones it can't stop</span></summary>

- **Radio-controlled FPVs,** which have no line. Jammers and the tripwire's warning are the answer there.
- **Fibre drones with a backup radio.** The cut forces them onto the radio, where the unit's jammers must take them down. With no jammer near, the cut does nothing.
- **A drone flying around the end** of the wire, or down a tree belt, or through a gate.
- **A drone already diving.** The cut comes too late to stop its momentum.
- **A line that crosses through the trees,** above the wire.
- **A pilot who sees the wire** and lands short of it, or another drone that cuts it. Like any fence, it shows where we are.

</details>

<details class="layer" id="what-has-to-be-tested" markdown="block">
<summary><span class="layer-title">What has to be tested</span><span class="layer-sub">Three tests that decide whether to build it</span></summary>

These are proposed, numbered C1–C3, apart from the tripwire's T-tests.

| Test | What | Pass | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1. Does the line land on the wire?** | Fly a drone that pays out a real FPV fibre, about 100 ft up, across a 3 ft wire on 165 ft spans over long grass. 20 passes, calm and in a 10–15 mph crosswind | The line lies across the wire, with no gap, in 18 of 20 passes | One spool, about $200–500, plus a borrowed fibre FPV kit |
| **C2. Does the trolley cut it?** | One span in a yard. Drape loose fibre across the wire, run the trolley at 20 mph, 100 times. Then 1,000 runs on fibre to wear the blade | 98 of 100 cut on the first pass. The blade still cuts after 1,000 | One trolley, about $110, plus $191 of wire and posts |
| **C3. Does it run through a winter?** | Two spans outside from December to March, sweeping hourly. Log every run, ice, snow and grass | 95% of runs reach the far end. The pack never runs flat | ~$300 |

**What would scrap it:** in C1, the line hangs above the wire or slides off it in wind most of the time. Or in C2, a V-notch can't reliably cut a line that lies loose, with no tension on it. The fallback is Chaharnyk's approach: drag hooks to pull the line tight before it breaks.

</details>
